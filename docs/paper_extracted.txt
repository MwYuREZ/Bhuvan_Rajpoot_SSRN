Does Discharge-Time Information Improve 30-Day Readmission Prediction?
A Patient-Disjoint Evaluation in the UCI Diabetes 130-US Hospitals Cohort
Bhuvan Rajpoot
Independent Researcher
21 July 2026
Abstract
Background: Readmission models are commonly evalu-
ated as if all electronic-record fields were simultaneously
available, although predictions made at admission and
at discharge answer different operational questions.Ob-
jective: To quantify the incremental predictive value of
information available by discharge over a compact set plau-
sibly available at admission for 30-day readmission among
inpatient encounters involving diabetes care.Methods:
A locked analysis used the public UCI Diabetes 130-US
Hospitals cohort. Death and hospice discharge pathways
and invalid gender records were excluded, leaving 99,340
encounters from 69,987 patients. Patients, not encoun-
ters, defined an 80/20 development/test split. Within
the development set, three-fold stratified group validation
compared prespecified L2 logistic regression and histogram
gradient boosting separately for 10 admission-time and
46 discharge-time predictors. Preprocessing and threshold
selection occurred inside development data. Discrimina-
tion, calibration, and F2 operating characteristics were
evaluated once in 19,942 encounters from 13,998 unseen
patients; 95% intervals used 500 patient-cluster bootstrap
replicates. Results: Logistic regression was selected for
admission-time data and gradient boosting for discharge-
time data. Held-out AUROC increased from 0.631 (95%
CI 0.615–0.646) to 0.677 (0.663–0.689), a paired difference
of 0.046 (0.036–0.056). Average precision increased from
0.206 to 0.237, a difference of 0.032 (0.020–0.042); outcome
prevalence was 11.52%. At the prespecified F2 threshold,
the discharge-time model achieved sensitivity 0.789 and
specificity 0.422, alerting on 60.2% of encounters.Con-
clusions: Encounter-complete information produced a
statistically clear but modest improvement. Performance
remained insufficient to justify clinical use without tem-
poral and external validation. Explicit predictor timing
and patient-disjoint evaluation should be treated as core
design choices, not implementation details.
Keywords: hospital readmission; diabetes; machine
learning; patient-disjoint validation; predictor availability;
calibration
1 Introduction
Thirty-day readmission is a difficult prognostic target. It
is shaped not only by diagnoses and treatment but also by
prior utilisation, discharge planning, social circumstances,
access to care, and events after discharge. Structured
electronic health records can support risk estimation, but
their fields become available at different points in the care
pathway. A model using length of stay, discharge disposi-
tion, final diagnoses, and medication changes cannot be
interpreted as an admission-time model merely because
those variables share a row in a retrospective table.
ThepublicDiabetes130-USHospitalsdatasetisawidely
used benchmark with 101,766 encounters collected from
130 US hospitals during 1999–2008 [1, 2]. The source
study examined the association between HbA1c measure-
ment and readmission [2]; subsequent prediction studies
have compared random forests, boosting, neural networks,
and regression [3, 4, 5]. These studies demonstrate the
benchmark’s difficulty, but a single “full feature” score
does not answer how much information is gained during
the encounter or what predictive performance is possible
earlier.
This study therefore asks a narrower question: how
much does information available by discharge add beyond
a compact admission-time view? Three design choices
follow from that question. First, predictor sets are defined
by approximate availability. Second, patients are kept
disjoint across every split so repeated encounters cannot
cross development and test partitions. Third, probability
quality and operational burden are reported alongside
ranking performance. Reporting follows the principles of
TRIPOD+AI[6], andrisk-of-biasconcernswereconsidered
with reference to PROBAST+AI [7].
2 Related work
Strack et al. assembled the cohort and analysed nearly
70,000 encounters with sufficient HbA1c detail, finding
that measurement patterns were associated with readmis-
sion [2]. Shang et al. later evaluated 23 predictors with an
80/20 split and found random forest to be the strongest of
three classifiers [3]. Emi-Johnson and Nkrumah compared
logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost, and a neural

These results place the attainable discrimination in con-

reported because ranking alone does not establish reliable
risk estimates [9].
3 Methods
3.1 Study design, data source, and ethics
This was a retrospective secondary analysis of de-identified,
publicly available data. UCI distributes the dataset under
CC BY 4.0 [1]. No attempt was made to identify indi-
viduals, and no new human participants were recruited;
institutional review board review was therefore not sought.
The analysis protocol (version 1.0) was written before the
newly seeded test partition was evaluated and is included
with the source archive.
The input file was required to match the following SHA-
256 checksum:
0689e7ec031237dc63031b938805c4837
7748761a3b26acab621567afa24df97
This guards against silent use of a modified mirror.
3.2 Cohort and outcome
The unit of analysis was an inpatient encounter. The
binary outcome was 1 whenreadmitted was <30 and 0
when it was >30 or NO. The label does not distinguish
planned from unplanned readmission. Rows with gender
Unknown/Invalid were removed. Discharge dispositions
11, 13, 14, 19, 20, and 21 were excluded because the
accompanying mapping identifies death or hospice path-
ways, for which ordinary opportunity for readmission is
not comparable. Question marks were treated as miss-
ing. weight was excluded because of extreme missingness.
encounter_id and patient_nbr were never predictors;
the latter was used only to keep patients disjoint.
Age intervals were replaced by their midpoints. The
three diagnosis fields were mapped, without outcome infor-
mation, to broad ICD-9 families (for example, circulatory,
respiratory, endocrine/metabolic, and injury/poisoning).
All other values retained their source meaning.
3.3 Predictor availability
The admission-time set contained 10 fields: race, gender,
age, admission type, admission source, payer code, medical
specialty, and counts of prior outpatient, emergency, and
inpatient visits. These represent demographics, admission
context, and prior utilisation.
The discharge-time set contained all 46 eligible pre-
dictors. It added discharge disposition, length of stay,
diagnoses, laboratory and procedure counts, medication
counts and states, glucose and HbA1c result categories,
and treatment-change indicators. “Discharge-time” means
available by the end of the encounter, not necessarily mea-
sured at the instant of discharge. Element- level times-
tamps were unavailable, so timing is an explicit approxi-
mation from field semantics.
3.4 Partitioning, preprocessing, and mod-
els
A grouped random split assigned 80% of patients to devel-
opment and 20% to the test partition (random state 2026).
Model selection used three-fold shuffled StratifiedGroupK-
Fold within development data (random state 2027). There
was no patient overlap across any assessment boundary.
Imputation and encoding were fitted separately in every
fold, preventing validation information from influencing
preprocessing.
Two model families were prespecified for each predictor
set:
1. L2-regularised logistic regression (C = 1 .0, LBFGS
solver, at most 1,000 iterations), using median impu-
tation and standardisation for numerical fields and
most-frequent imputation plus one-hot encoding for
categorical fields.
2. Histogram gradient boosting (200 iterations, learning
rate 0.05, 31 leaf nodes, minimum 30 samples per leaf,
L2 regularisation 1.0, and early stopping disabled),
using median imputation for numerical fields and
ordinal encoding with an explicit unknown value for
categorical fields.
No synthetic sampling, class weighting, or post-hoc fea-
ture selection was used. For each availability set, the
candidate with the highest development out-of-fold aver-
age precision was selected; lower Brier score broke ties.
This allows model complexity to differ when warranted
by development evidence while keeping the feature-timing
comparison prespecified. Models were implemented in
scikit-learn [10].
3.5 Performance and statistical analysis
The held-out test set was used once after selection. Re-
ported probability metrics were AUROC, average precision
(AP), Brier score, log loss, calibration intercept, and cal-
ibration slope. Calibration curves used equal-frequency
deciles. Intercept 0 and slope 1 indicate ideal weak cali-
bration [9].

F1- and F2-maximising thresholds were chosen from
development out-of-fold predictions. F2 was the primary
operating point, weighting recall more strongly. At each
threshold, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value
(PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), F1, F2, and alert
rate were calculated. These are descriptive operating
scenarios, not recommended clinical cut-offs.
Uncertainty used 500 nonparametric bootstrap repli-
cates sampled at patient level, preserving all encounters
for a sampled patient. The primary contrast was the
paired discharge-minus-admission difference in AUROC,
AP, and Brier score on the same resampled patients. Per-
centile 95% intervals are reported. Performance by gender
and race was descriptive for groups with at least 200 test
encounters and both outcome classes; it is not a fairness
certification.
4 Results
4.1 Cohort and partition integrity
Of 101,766 source encounters, 2,426 were excluded, leav-
ing 99,340 encounters from 69,987 patients. There were
11,314 positive encounters (11.39%). The test set con-
tained 19,942 encounters from 13,998 patients and had
prevalence 11.52%; no patient appeared in both develop-
ment and test data (Table 1).

4.2 Model selection and held-out perfor-
mance
Development AP selected logistic regression for the
admission-time set (AP 0.194) and gradient boosting for
the discharge-time set (AP 0.220). Both choices were
fixed before test evaluation. Held-out results for every
prespecified candidate are shown in Table 2; the selected
comparison is visualised in Figures 1–3.
The discharge-time model improved AUROC by 0.046
(95%CI0.036–0.056)andAPby0.032(0.020–0.042). Brier
score decreased by 0.0022 (95% CI -0.0028 to -0.0016 for
the signed discharge-minus-admission difference), indicat-
ing a small improvement in probability accuracy. Calibra-
tion was broadly reasonable: the admission and discharge
calibration intercepts were -0.173 and 0.103, with slopes
0.913 and 1.046, respectively.
4.3 Operating points and subgroup de-
scription
At the primary F2 threshold selected in development,
admission-time prediction reached sensitivity 0.844 and
specificity 0.264, but would flag 74.8% of test encounters.
The discharge-time model reached sensitivity 0.789 and
specificity 0.422 while flagging 60.2% (Table 3). The
reduction in alert burden illustrates why a discrimination
gain can matter operationally, but a 60.2% alert rate
remains too broad to imply readiness for deployment.

5 Discussion
5.1 Principal findings
Information accumulated during the encounter improved
30-day readmission prediction over admission-time context
and prior utilisation alone. The paired increase of approx-
imately 0.046 in AUROC and 0.032 in AP was unlikely
to be a bootstrap sampling artefact. The discharge-time
model also reduced Brier score and retained a calibration
slope close to one. Nevertheless, absolute performance
remained moderate, with AP only about twice the event
prevalence.
The comparison clarifies two distinct tasks. Admission-
time prediction may give teams more time to organise care
but has less information. Discharge-time prediction can
incorporate the course of the stay and final disposition,
but offers a shorter intervention window. It is therefore
misleading to rank these models without naming their
prediction time. The present results quantify that trade-
off on a common holdout rather than treating feature
availability as an afterthought.
The discharge AUROC of 0.677 is consistent with the
0.667reportedforXGBoostbyEmi-JohnsonandNkrumah
[4] and with the moderate performance of more extensive
recent evaluations [5]. This convergence points to a ceiling
imposed by the benchmark’s structured variables; algo-
rithms alone cannot recover unrecorded social support, dis-
ease trajectory, follow-up access, or post-discharge events.

5.2 Operational interpretation
Threshold selection exposes the cost of pursuing sensitivity
in an imbalanced problem. The discharge F2 operating
point detected 78.9% of positive encounters but flagged
60.2% of all encounters and had PPV 15.1%. Such a
threshold might support a very low-cost secondary review,
not a scarce intensive intervention. Clinical usefulness
depends on intervention benefit, capacity, harms, and cost;
none were measured here. Decision-curve or prospective
impact analysis would be needed before recommending a
threshold.
The probability estimates were reasonably calibrated
internally, but calibration commonly shifts between insti-
tutions and over time [9]. Any future use would require
local recalibration, prospective monitoring, and prede-
fined action pathways. Subgroup results should also be
re-estimated with sufficiently large contemporary samples
before any equity claim.
5.3 Strengths and limitations
Strengths include a protocol locked before the new hold-
out was reviewed, exact source-data verification, patient-
disjoint partitions, fold-local preprocessing, development-
only threshold choice, paired cluster uncertainty, calibra-
tion reporting, and complete code and machine-readable
outputs. Testing two prespecified model families avoided
a broad post-hoc search.
Several limitations are decisive. First, the data are
historical (1999–2008), and treatment, coding, discharge
processes, and population composition have changed. Sec-
ond, field-level timestamps are absent; availability sets
are based on semantics and may not match every hospi-
tal workflow. Third, the outcome does not distinguish
planned from unplanned readmission, and readmissions
outsidethecontributingdatanetworkmaynotbecaptured.
Fourth, hospital and calendar identifiers are unavailable,
preventing site-held-out or temporal validation. Fifth,
structured fields omit social determinants, functional sta-
tus, clinician judgement, and post-discharge access. Sixth,
the test partition is internal to the same retrospective
source; bootstrap intervals measure sampling uncertainty,
not transportability. Finally, the subgroup analysis is
descriptive and thin for several categories.
6 Conclusion
In this patient-disjoint study, information available by
discharge yielded a clear but modest improvement over
admission-time information for predicting a recorded read-
mission within 30 days. The result supports explicit defi-
nition of prediction time and shows that development-only
threshold selection and calibration reporting materially
change interpretation. The models are reproducible re-
search benchmarks, not clinical devices. Contemporary
temporal, external, and prospective validation is required
before any patient-care use.
